+7 m The organizing
idea

The central thesis

One spec, three disasters

The hurricane spec and the fortress spec are the same spec.

Impact glazing rated for Category 5 debris is already multi-layer laminate; thicken the interlayer and you have rifle resistance with identical sightlines. Motorized storm shutters are motorized security shutters. ICF with an eight-inch reinforced core stops a hurricane-borne 2×4 at 100 mph and most of what a rifle does. The seismic detailing you need anyway produces progressive-collapse resistance for free.

Nothing has to look like a bunker, because the bunker is already load-bearing.

The conflict the design resolves

Hurricane wants you sealed — mass, shutters, no openings. Tsunami wants the opposite at ground level: a solid wall facing a wave is a wall that gets pushed over, so you let the water pass under the building.

The resolution is to split them by elevation. Seaward structures are open at grade and sealed above; everything hardened lives inland and high. Band B's ground level is carport, boat storage, outdoor kitchen and breezeway — designed to flood and be hosed out. Living space starts twelve to fifteen feet up on a concrete deck.

What the flood map actually says

That reasoning is sound but, on this parcel, largely unnecessary. FEMA's VE zone — the one with wave action, base flood elevation 3.4 m — stops eight metres inland of the waterline, and the tsunami evacuation zone stops at twelve. Both are seaward of the road. The buildable land begins at +7.2 m, in Zone X, outside both. The sacrificial ground floor is a good idea here, not a required one, and the document should say which.

Verification

What the survey data changed

This design was written from listing copy and satellite imagery. Before drawing any of it, the parcel was checked against primary sources: USGS elevation, the Planning Board's zoning and hazard layers, FEMA's flood mapping and NOAA's tidal datums. Six of the twelve open questions closed. Three of the answers change the design.

The lots are exactly what the listings said

The four solares are surveyed at 8,000.1571, 8,000.0006, 8,000.0005 and 8,061.2339 m² — 32,061.39 m², or 7.92 acres. The "approximately 2 acres each" figure and the "8,000 m² each" figure were never in conflict; 8,000 m² is 1.98 acres. There was no unit error to find.

The zoning is real, and it is not where the pin was

The district exists: DTS — Distrito Turístico Selectivo, on Suelo Rústico Común, catastro 429-000-006-01, barrio Montalva, under resolution JP-ZIT-71-72-01. The listings' shorthand "DT" is close enough.

But the coordinate this design was written around — 17.9488° N, 66.9130° W — is not in it. That point falls in B-Q, Bosque, classified SREP-E, Suelo Rústico Especialmente Protegido — Ecológico: the protected forest buffer of the Guánica biosphere reserve. The DTS land sits about 1.4 km west-southwest. Every drawing here uses the DTS parcel.

The waterfront is not yours to build on

This is the finding that actually binds. The design opens with Band A — the palapa, the fire pit, the boat ramp, the sea grape berm, "the paradise" — sitting on the water. On the real parcel that strip is zoned CR, Conservación de Recursos, and the road corridor runs between it and the buildable land. The DTS parcel does not reach the sea. It begins forty-four metres inland, at +7.2 m, behind a public road.

That does not kill the compound; it moves one band of it onto land the owner would have to negotiate for rather than buy. But it is the difference between a waterfront property and a property with a view, and no amount of design resolves it.

The Zona Marítimo-Terrestre, mapped

Puerto Rico inherited Spanish civil law here: the maritime-terrestrial zone is public domain, full stop, and its landward edge is set by the reach of the largest storm waves. MIPR does carry an official reference line — the Línea de Referencia Oficial — and it runs this coast. It is a reference, not a survey. Only a DRNA deslinde fixes the real boundary, and on a parcel whose seaward third is already conservation-zoned, that survey decides more here than the zoning table does.

The hazards are smaller than the design assumed

The 2020 M6.4 sequence was centred essentially at this location and the Muertos Trough lies directly offshore, so the tsunami case is real. But the Planning Board's evacuation zone reaches only +3.1 m, twelve metres inland, and FEMA's VE zone with its 3.4 m base flood elevation stops at eight. The headland is steep enough that the water has nowhere to run.

Everything hardened in this design already sits above +7 m and outside both lines. The vertical evacuation refuge on the roof terrace remains a sensible thing to own. It is no longer the thing the building is for.

the public strip

Band 0 — 0 to 44 m

The land you look across

Between the parcel and the sea there are three separate claims, and the owner holds none of them. Nearest the water is the Zona Marítimo-Terrestre. Behind it, forty-odd metres of land zoned CR — Conservación de Recursos. Behind that, the road.

The maritime-terrestrial zone

Puerto Rico inherited Spanish civil law here. The ZMT is public domain, full stop, and its landward edge is set by the reach of the largest storm waves — not by a fixed contour, and not by anything the owner can move. DRNA fixes it with a survey called a deslinde. People may legally walk the shoreline. MIPR publishes an official reference line for this coast, but a reference line is not a deslinde, and until one is run nobody can say precisely where the public domain ends.

What this deletes: the boat ramp, the davit, the beach palapa, the sea grape berm as a wave-energy dissipator, and the idea of a private shoreline. What it leaves: an uninterrupted view south over the reef flats, across land that by law will never be built on. That is not nothing. A conservation strip and a public road in front of you is a guarantee no covenant could buy.

Privacy therefore faces inland, not seaward. The exposure is the road, forty-four metres away and slightly below. Screening it is a berm-and-planting problem on the parcel's own front edge, which is where the sea grape and the columnar cactus go — doing the same job the brief gave them, aimed the other way.

Where the boat goes

Guánica Bay is a deep, protected natural harbour about a mile away, and the brief already identified it as the real answer. With no shore access of your own, it becomes the only answer: a slip or a mooring in the bay, and the boathouse on the parcel reduced to what it should have been anyway — a workshop with a trailer in it.

This costs less than it sounds. After a hurricane the roads are debris fields and the water is often the clearest route on the island; a capable centre console in Guánica Bay reaches Ponce or La Parguera without touching a road either way. The mile of separation only matters on calm days, and on calm days it does not matter.

Band A — 44 to 100 m

The terrace

The parcel begins at +7.2 m, immediately behind the road, and stays nearly flat for fifty metres before the slope picks up. That flat is the outdoor room: palapa, fire pit, outdoor shower, long table, and the view south. Everything here is expendable by design — but the threat it is expendable to is wind, not water. At +7 m in Zone X it is outside the VE zone and outside the tsunami evacuation zone both.

Nothing buried in this band except re-pullable conduit. The live-fire propane grill lives here, above ground, for reasons the club section explains at length.

What the dry forest gives you free

This is subtropical dry forest: limestone, thorn scrub, columnar cactus, sea grape, guayacán. It is brown much of the year under hard white light, and it looks more like Baja than San Juan. Landscape here is not a maintenance item — the native planting is drought-adapted, needs no irrigation off a 50,000-gallon cistern, and is genuinely impassable. The property line plants itself.

+9.0 → +18.0 m Band B
the house

Band B — 100 to 190 m

The house

Main house about 4,500 sq ft over three levels, with the guest casita on its own lot seaward of it and the pool behind.

Passive design — invert the mainland instincts

The job here is shedding heat, not trapping it. Long axis east–west, so the two large faces look north and south. The building pierced so the easterly trades blow straight through the living space. Deep concrete brise-soleil on the south face. Flat or single-slope white roof.

In the tropics humidity is the comfort driver, not temperature. Design around dehumidification: high-SEER inverter VRF, aggressive shading, night-flush ventilation. A house that holds 78°F at 55% relative humidity feels better than one holding 72°F at 75%, and costs a fraction as much to run.

Structure

  • ICF, with serious seismic confinement detailing at columns and joints — not just thick walls. The January 2020 M6.4 sequence was centred essentially here.
  • Corrosion-resistant reinforcement. Salt air eats ordinary rebar through concrete, and this is the failure mode that takes Caribbean concrete buildings down thirty years early.
  • Impact glazing throughout; motorized storm and security shutters on every opening.
  • Multi-point locking on exterior doors, steel frames welded to embedded plates, concealed hinges.

Spaces

Ground level is sacrificial by design — carport, boat and trailer storage, outdoor kitchen, breezeway. Living levels above, reached by one stair and one pneumatic elevator, both closable: a hard interior line that costs nothing extra. Roof terrace, commercial-grade main kitchen, and the library with the concealed bookcase door.

What the flood map did to the elevated ground floor

The sacrificial ground level was the design's answer to the tsunami case: let the water pass under the building rather than push on a wall. At +9 m, six metres above the tsunami evacuation limit and well outside the VE zone, no code requires it and no modelled wave reaches it. Keep it anyway — a carport and a breezeway is what you want at grade in this climate regardless, and the same open frame that sheds a wave also sheds a storm surge nobody modelled. It is now a good idea rather than a governing constraint, and the difference is worth stating plainly.

The pool

Behind the house, so building mass screens it from the road. Saltwater chlorination. No heating. It quietly becomes infrastructure: thermal mass, fire reserve, and about 20,000 gallons of flushing water after a storm.

A wellness pavilion — sauna, cold plunge, hot tub — rebuilt in concrete. And the guest casita on its own lot, for genuine separation rather than a spare bedroom with a different door.

−3.4 m Below grade
the gallery

The tunnels — about 900 linear feet

Cut and cover, never mined

You trench, build a reinforced concrete box, waterproof it, and backfill. Mining into karst limestone risks hitting solution voids and costs roughly three times as much. On a headland made of exactly that limestone, the method chooses itself.

Eight feet wide by eight feet tall inside. Minimum three feet of cover, more where shielding matters. Never flat — everything grades to a drain, with sumps at low points and gravity daylighting where the slope allows. On this parcel the slope allows a lot: eight per cent, steadily downhill toward the sea, which means the whole gallery drains the way you want without a single lift pump in the critical path.

The sally port needs somebody else's permission

The sea tunnel was designed to descend from the house and daylight through the bluff face onto the beach terrace — above water, gravity-drained, closed at the uphill end by a marine watertight bulkhead door. The engineering is right, and the reason for it is right: anything at or below the water table on limestone becomes a hull you pump forever, so the tunnel must daylight rather than dive.

But it would now have to pass under a public road and surface on conservation land inside the maritime-terrestrial zone. That is an easement and a DRNA permit, not a construction detail. Build the run to the parcel boundary; treat the last forty metres as contingent on permissions nobody can assume.

Why the tunnels justify themselves without any drama

Every run of power, water, data and RO piping lives on cable tray in the gallery. In salt air, direct-buried conduit corrodes and fails, and then you are excavating a landscape to fix a wire. A serviceable gallery pays for itself on maintenance alone, and would be worth building on a property with no shelter, no club and no threat model at all.

Napa wine cave, not missile silo.

Barrel-vaulted, lime-plastered, warm cove lighting, arched alcoves for wine and storage. A guest walking to the casita after dinner should think this is the most beautiful part of the property. That is not decoration — it is the difference between infrastructure people avoid and infrastructure people use, and a tunnel nobody enjoys walking is a tunnel nobody walks.

The centrepiece — about 4,500 sq ft, buried

The Club

A guest is having dinner in the main house. The owner gets up, does something unremarkable to a bookcase, and a section of the library wall swings. Behind it, a stair descends into barrel-vaulted plaster with warm cove lighting. They walk four hundred and forty feet through that — long enough to stop being a doorway trick and start being a journey — and it opens into a room with two lit bowling lanes, a bar, and a game already going.

The door is only half of it. The tunnel is the other half.

The distance is what sells it — and the survey doubled it. The design was drawn for a two-hundred-foot walk on a 140 m lot. The real parcel is 240 m deep, which pushes the club to roughly a hundred and thirty metres from the library: about a minute and a half underground, at walking pace, before the room opens up. That is past the point where a guest is still counting it as a trick.

The lanes — about 1,400 sq ft

  • Two synthetic lanes. Wood is maple and pine and moves with humidity; non-viable in coastal Puerto Rico.
  • String pinsetters, not free-fall. Free-fall pinsetters are elaborate machines needing a service technician. This is a headland in southwest Puerto Rico. String pinsetters have a fraction of the moving parts and can be cleared with a ladder and ten minutes. Same logic as everything else here: choose the system you can fix alone.
  • Locally hosted scoring, not cloud-dependent. An amenity that bricks when Starlink drops fails exactly when it is needed.
  • Floated lane substructure, decoupled from the shelter and clinic. Pin decks put real vibration into rock.

The games hall — about 1,200 sq ft

  • Nine-foot regulation pool table with proper cue clearance
  • Twenty-two-foot tournament shuffleboard
  • Air hockey
  • Two dart lanes with real oche distance and a throw line nobody is standing in

Sightlines arranged so no game blocks any other. Learned the hard way on an earlier version of this design, where the shuffleboard ended up in front of the television.

The bar, the kitchen, the back of house

The bar sits at the junction so it serves the lanes and the game floor from one position. Soda gun on bag-in-box syrup, long-draw glycol draft lines from the walk-in, ice machine, undercounter refrigeration, dedicated glasswasher. Plus kitchen and lounge, and behind them the walk-in, dry storage and mechanical.

Cooking underground is the actual engineering problem

1 — Propane is heavier than air

In a buried room a leak does not rise and dissipate. It pools on the floor and waits. That is an explosive atmosphere in a confined space with one theatrical entrance. Therefore the underground kitchen is all-electric: induction, an electric deck oven that holds 800°F for real pizza, an electric fryer, a plancha. The live-fire grill stays above ground in Band A.

2 — CO₂ is also heavier than air

Bulk CO₂ for the soda gun and draft system is an asphyxiation hazard in a basement, and a real code item in many jurisdictions. Floor-level CO₂ and LP detection with alarms, hard-interlocked to the exhaust.

3 — Grease demands a commercial hood

Type I hood over the cooking line, welded liquid-tight grease duct with cleanouts running all the way to the surface, wet-chemical suppression, and — the part people forget — tempered makeup air, because you cannot exhaust 2,000 CFM out of a sealed box without replacing it. In Guánica that makeup air arrives at 88°F and 80% humidity and must be dehumidified before it enters.

4 — Grease interceptor

Ahead of the septic system, or grease kills the aerobic treatment unit and the drip field within a year.

The door, engineered

Counterbalanced pivot on a heavy bookcase, electric release with a concealed trigger. Mechanical override from both sides, always — a power-dependent door in a compound built around outages is a trap, not a feature.

And it is not the only exit. A 4,500 sq ft space with a commercial kitchen needs real egress. The tunnel gallery to Band C provides a second, and the shelter's escape shaft a third. The secret door is the arrival experience; the code exits exist, marked and discreet, and will never be used.

The range

Fifty metres, two lanes, underground

Off the gallery, cut deeper than the rest of it — more rock overhead is the cheapest sound isolation there is, and the excavation is already open. Extending cut-and-cover is the same crew and the same method.

A surface range is impossible here. Eight acres on a headland means every orientation points at a public shoreline, boaters, the road, or your own buildings. Underground is not a preference; it is the only geometry that closes.

  • Granulated rubber backstop, not steel — self-healing, no ricochet, far quieter, and simpler lead reclamation. Ballistic ceiling baffles against uprange ricochet.
  • Ventilation: laminar downrange airflow at 50–75 fpm from behind the firing line. About 7,000 CFM for two lanes, HEPA-filtered exhaust, tempered makeup air. 15–25 kW while running — the single largest load on the compound.
  • Sealed epoxy floor sloped to a sealed sump. HEPA-vacuumed, never swept.
  • Acoustic absorption throughout, or the interior is punishing.
  • A briefing room off the range gallery, dual-purposed as a media room.
  • A trauma station at the firing line.

The range cannot be hydraulically connected to anything

There is a brackish well feeding a reverse-osmosis system on the same parcel, on karst limestone, above a freshwater lens. Lead reaching groundwater here is not a containable mistake. Sealed floor, sealed sump, documented lead reclamation on a schedule, and no shared drainage with any other system on the property.

The simulator is where the skill actually comes from

Projection plus laser: unlimited repetitions at zero ammunition, zero lead, zero ventilation load — and it runs on about 400 W during a lockdown, when the live range cannot run at all. Realistically this is where most competence accrues.

Force-on-force: the compound is the shoot house. Running marking-round scenarios through the actual floor plan — the real stairwell, the real tunnel move, the real shelter door — is worth more than any generic structure.

Needs Puerto Rico–specific legal review on private-property discharge rules, and on how insurance changes the moment a non-household member shoots there. Before anything is poured.

The armory

Salt, not burglary

Located in Band C off the gallery, between the workshop and the shelter. Not inside the shelter — you do not want a magazine in the room you are sealed into for thirty days. Plus a small biometric ready-safe in the master.

Coastal air will destroy blued steel faster than any intruder.

  • Separately conditioned: 35–45% RH held steady
  • Dedicated dehumidifier with desiccant backup that functions at zero watts
  • VCI emitters, positive filtered pressure
  • Open racks, never soft cases — cases trap moisture against metal

Why the powder magazine wants wooden cabinets

Smokeless powder deflagrates rather than detonates — unless you confine it. A steel safe packed with powder becomes a pressure vessel. Fire codes specify wooden cabinets precisely so it burns instead of bursting. Propellant and primers live in a separate vented magazine room in wood cabinetry, well away from both the vault and the 300 kWh battery bank.

Inside: vertical long-gun racks muzzle-down, pistol drawers, optics cabinet, reloading bench, records cabinet — Puerto Rico registration means licences, serials and insurance schedules all want to be in one place — and a cleaning bench with dedicated exhaust to outside plus a sink, because lead exposure is a genuine health issue in an enclosed room.

Entry: a UL-rated vault door, not a gun safe. Dual-factor biometric and PIN, logged. Behind a concealed panel in the workshop wall.

Legal footing

  • El Pueblo v. Rodríguez López (PR Supreme Court, October 2022) confirmed the Second Amendment is a fundamental right extended to Puerto Rico, while upholding the licensing requirement.
  • The Weapons Act of 2020 made Puerto Rico shall-issue, folded possession and carry into a single licence, and added carry reciprocity and stand-your-ground.
  • Roughly $200 for a five-year licence, $100 to renew. Training required before issuance. No assault weapon law.
  • Purchases and registration go through a police registry.
  • Mainland credentials do not travel — reciprocity depends on MOUs the Police Commissioner must sign.

Active legislation has been moving in this area; verify current status before relying on any of it.

The clinic

The equipment is the easy part

Almost anything can be bought on the secondary market. What money cannot buy is the licence to use it. So the design question is not "what can I install" but "what capability actually exists at 3 a.m. when someone is bleeding."

Tier 1 — fully yours, and it covers about 95% of reality

  • Hemorrhage: CAT tourniquets, hemostatic gauze, chest seals, pressure dressings — staged at the range, the workshop, every vehicle, not just the clinic.
  • Airway: supraglottic airways (i-gel) rather than intubation kits, because they are dramatically more forgiving in untrained hands. BVM, suction, oxygen.
  • Diagnostics, where the money earns out: a point-of-care ultrasound is the single highest-value item in the room. No radiation, no licensing burden; finds internal bleeding, pneumothorax, fractures, cardiac problems. Pair it with a handheld blood analyzer for chemistry, blood gas, lactate and troponin. Those two turn "I have no idea what is wrong" into an answer.
  • Monitoring, 12-lead ECG, AED, splints, pelvic binder, IV and intraosseous access, fluids, suture and stapling kits, autoclave.
  • Oxygen concentrators over cylinders — they make O₂ from air with electricity instead of depleting a finite supply. The correct answer for a compound with 300 kWh of battery.

Tier 2 — buildable, usable only with licensed hands present

OR table, surgical lighting, electrocautery, anesthesia machine, laminar-flow HEPA at 20+ air changes with positive pressure, and isolated ungrounded power with a line-isolation monitor, which is genuine operating-room electrical code. Build it — it is real capability the day a physician stands in it. Just do not mistake owning it for having it.

Tier 3 — what money cannot buy

  • Controlled substances. Real anesthesia and serious analgesia require DEA registration, which requires a licence to practice. There is no private path.
  • Blood. Red cells last about six weeks. The remote-medicine answer is a walking blood bank — household members pre-typed and screened, fresh whole blood drawn on demand. That requires kit and training, not inventory.
  • A surgical team.

The move that makes it real

Retain a physician as medical director, backed by 24/7 telemedicine. Not exotic — it is how offshore platforms, expedition ships and superyachts operate. A medical director can legally prescribe and stock the pharmacy, write standing protocols, and be on video in minutes. That single retainer converts a room of expensive props into an actual clinic, and it is the highest-leverage line item in this section by an enormous margin.

Pharmacy stocked toward what actually kills people in remote settings: broad-spectrum antibiotics, tranexamic acid, epinephrine, anti-emetics, cardiac drugs, non-controlled analgesics. Infection and untreated bleeding, not exotic trauma.

Placement: Band C, off the gallery, sharing a wall with the shelter so it is reachable fully sealed. Shares the shelter's air plant. Dedicated UPS branch with generator priority above every other load. Monitored, logged pharmacy refrigeration with backup.

The real backbone is evacuation, not surgery.

Ponce is thirty to forty minutes by road with hospitals; Centro Médico in San Juan is the island's main trauma centre. An air-ambulance membership and a pre-briefed, lit landing zone will save more lives than the operating room ever will.

And the part that is not a purchase: Wilderness EMT or equivalent for the owner, Stop the Bleed for every guest, annual recurrent training. A world-class facility with an untrained operator produces confident incompetence, which is more dangerous than no facility at all.

The shelter

A four-week autonomy module

Air plant: HEPA plus activated carbon, slight positive pressure so leakage goes outward, and a hand-crank blower so it works at zero watts.

The binding constraints are filter media and morale — not power, water or food. Stock three or four complete filter changes.

Therefore the shelter is designed like a small hotel, not a submarine: daylight tubes, real ventilation, decent headroom, and separate spaces rather than one bunk room. Eight bunks, galley, air plant, stores, comms, Faraday cage. Entry through a concealed door at the back of the workshop.

Never one door. A concealed vertical escape shaft daylights well away from everything, its hatch buried in landscape.

The nuclear section, written honestly

Puerto Rico is not a plausible target. The realistic scenarios are fallout drifting from elsewhere, an EMP event, or disruption with no direct effect at all — and only the first needs a shelter.

The shielding math is simple. Gamma attenuation has a halving thickness of roughly 2.4 inches through concrete and about 3.6 inches through soil. Every halving thickness cuts dose in half. A protection factor over 1,000 takes about ten halvings — roughly two feet of concrete or three feet of earth. A shelter cut into limestone with several feet of rock overhead exceeds that without trying. The geology does the work.

The 7:10 rule: for every sevenfold increase in time after detonation, dose rate drops tenfold. Seven hours → one tenth. Two days → one hundredth. Two weeks → one thousandth. So the sheltering window is two to four weeks —

— which is exactly the post-hurricane window. This is not a nuclear bunker with hurricane benefits. It is a four-week autonomy module, and fallout is one of several reasons you might need it.

María left parts of Puerto Rico without power for months. The provisioning, water, fuel, air handling and sanity requirements are identical.

Power

Split five ways on purpose

60 kW DC across five separate subarrays, each on its own MPPT: house roof, casita roof, workshop roof, carport canopy, and the Band C ground array. The splitting is the point — a hurricane that takes one does not take the system.

At about 5.5 peak sun hours: roughly 260 kWh/day. A full-luxury day draws 100 to 120. Deliberately at about 2× headroom.

  • Fixed, low-tilt, roof-integrated, hurricane-rated racking. No tracker.
  • Mechanically fastened through to concrete, never ballasted. Marine-grade racking, salt-mist-certified modules.
  • A crated spare array in the shelter — enough panels and a portable ground frame to restore critical power in an afternoon if the roofs are stripped.

Storage: 300 kWh usable LFP in the Band C vault, plus a 40 kWh isolated life-support bank inside the shelter on its own bus, so a fault or flood in the main bank cannot strand you. Multiple parallel inverters, never one large one.

Backup: 45 kW diesel, 2,500 gallons buried, with a fuel-polishing loop, because diesel degrades in storage. Propane separately for above-ground cooking.

A better job for the same brain

The automation layer runs battery dispatch, the RO unit on excess PV only, load-shedding, and automated storm-prep sequencing. The panel-angle showpiece was lost with the tracker; this is a more useful job for the same controller, and it is the difference between a system that has 2× headroom and one that uses it.

EMP: panels themselves are fairly resilient; the electronics are the weak link. So the answer is a Faraday cage with spare charge controllers, inverter boards, optimizers, radios, a laptop and the sat phone — not hardening everything.

Water

The genuinely hard problem

Guánica is the driest place in Puerto Rico — about 33 inches a year, and it does not arrive evenly. A May pulse, a main season from August to November, then roughly five dry months. Water is harder here than power by a wide margin.

Layer 1 — catchment

About 8,000 sq ft of roof and canopy × 33 in/yr at 80% efficiency ≈ 130,000 gallons a year, or 360 gallons a day averaged. The averaging is the lie in that sentence; see the five dry months.

Layer 2 — storage

50,000 gallons of buried concrete cistern under the Band C terrace, sized to carry Christmas to May.

Layer 3 — brackish reverse osmosis

A well here will probably not give fresh water. The South Coast aquifer has been compromised by saltwater intrusion, and on a narrow limestone headland the best case is a thin freshwater lens floating on seawater.

Which is exactly what you want.

Brackish-water RO runs at a fraction of the pressure and energy of seawater RO. A well that "fails" as a drinking source succeeds beautifully as a desalination feed — stable temperature, pre-filtered through rock, almost no biofouling. This is a beach well intake, which is what serious small-scale desalination installations use instead of an open ocean pipe.

What a watermaker actually does

Seawater is about 35,000 ppm dissolved solids; drinking water needs to be under 500. A high-pressure pump forces seawater against a semipermeable membrane at 800 to 1,000 psi, hard enough to push water molecules through while salt ions cannot follow. About 10% comes out drinkable; the rest returns as brine.

Energy recovery is the clever part. That rejected 90% is still at nearly full pressure, so a pressure intensifier — Spectra's Clark pump is the well-known one — uses the outgoing brine to help pressurize the incoming feed, cutting consumption several-fold. That is the entire reason a watermaker can run off a solar array instead of a generator. Names in the space: Spectra (efficiency leader, cruiser favourite), Sea Recovery (larger and commercial), Rainman (portable, deliberately simple), Katadyn (tiny emergency hand units).

Energy: about 10 to 15 kWh per 1,000 gallons. At 150 gal/day that is under 2 kWh — nothing against an array making 260. The cost is membranes and discipline: a watermaker that sits idle grows things, so it needs regular flushing, and pickling if stored.

Layer 4 — trucked water

A completely normal service in the dry southwest and after storms. The backstop, not the plan. Cisterns are sized so one truck fill carries weeks.

Plus the pool as reserve, and composting heads in the shelter so a lockdown consumes essentially no water. At eight people, about 40 gal/day against 50,000 gallons is roughly three years.

The cross-link that kills a crop

Hydroponics requires low-TDS water. You cannot run it on brackish well water — sodium accumulates in a recirculating system and kills the crop. It must be rainwater or RO permeate, which this architecture already produces. Plumb it that way from the start; it is almost impossible to retrofit once the lines are buried.

The road cuts both ways

A public road forty-four metres from the front door is the one security change the survey forced. It removes the private beach, and with it the "approach by boat, land on your sand" scenario the marine radar was partly for. It adds a permanent, lawful stream of strangers passing at close range — which is exactly the condition Layer 0 was written for. Looking boring stops being a preference and becomes the primary control.

Movement

Do not plan to leave during

Plan not to need to.

Thirty days of autonomy exist specifically so the dangerous window passes at home. Egress is for after, in daylight, on your schedule. Ranked: shelter in place, then the boat, then a boring truck, then a helicopter.

The boring truck

A conspicuous new vehicle is a liability — but the fix is not "no truck." It is a fifteen-year-old Hilux or 4Runner with faded paint, a dent you never fixed, and a completely rebuilt drivetrain underneath. A common model, so parts exist on the island and a mechanic in Yauco can fix it. Standard practice for NGO and expat operations in exactly these conditions. About $40K against a $22M compound.

The boat

After a hurricane the roads are debris fields, but the water is often the clearest route on the island and nobody blocks it. Guánica Bay is a deep, protected natural harbour about a mile away — and, since the survey took the private shoreline away, the only place the boat can live.

Why the helicopter cannot be primary

Rotary inventory on the island is a small pool. In a real event every airframe is spoken for by government, medical and relief priority — after María, aviation assets were saturated for weeks. Add weather minimums, night limits, and the fact that nothing flies during the storm.

Import

The Port of Ponce (deep water, forty minutes) or Guánica Bay. Puerto Rico is domestic — no customs, just Jones Act coastwise shipping from the mainland. Heavy materials come by container to Ponce and up the road. Helicopters are dreadful freight, about 3,000 lb at thousands per hour. And improve the access road, which is entirely within the owner's control and trivial against this budget.

Your neighbours are an asset, not a threat

Separate "the roads are bad" from "the neighbours are dangerous." The first is verifiable from satellite. The second is not.

Disaster research is remarkably consistent that post-event behaviour skews prosocial, and that fear of predation causes more bad decisions than predation does. Post-María, the dominant pattern in rural Puerto Rico was mutual aid. Meanwhile the security literature on remote property is unanimous that your neighbours are your most valuable asset: they notice the unfamiliar truck, they call you, they watch the place in August. A compound that reads as fortified-against-the-locals forfeits that and buys nothing. Which is exactly what Layer 0 already said — extend it to how you show up in town.

Aviation

The pad, and the one route that pays for it

On the Band C high ground, set back from the bluff edge, because easterly trades rolling over a bluff face produce mechanical turbulence exactly where an approach ends.

  • Sized for a twin (H145 / AW109) so charter operators can actually use it, and because twins are what you want over water: 40×40 TLOF, 70×70 FATO, plus safety area, slab engineered for dynamic loading well above max gross weight.
  • Perimeter lighting, lighted wind cone, pilot-controlled lighting for night ops.
  • Governed by FAA AC 150/5390-2; file FAA Form 7480-1 before construction, plus PR Ports Authority permitting.
  • Site advantage: a headland gives two obstacle-free approach paths straight off the water. Almost no private helipad gets that.
  • Rotor downwash will destroy the ground-mounted solar array. Keep the pad well clear of it, the pool, and any loose landscape.
  • 1,500-gallon Jet A farm with spill containment. Jet A is a kerosene and, with lubricity additive, will run the diesel generator — aviation fuel and emergency power become mutually redundant.

San Juan to New York is about 1,570 nm — light jets cannot do it nonstop. It needs super-midsize: Challenger 350, Citation Longitude, Praetor 600. For ten to fifteen trips a year a jet card is the right instrument; fractional needs about 50 hrs/yr and whole ownership 200+. Roughly 25 hours on a super-mid card, $300–400K.

Verify Puerto Rico service-area coverage before signing

Many card programs define their primary service area as the continental US and treat Puerto Rico as extra-area with surcharges, or exclude it outright. This is the single most likely place a program disappoints you.

The whole thing is domestic — no customs, no passports, no international handling. The route this is really for: compound to Isla Grande by helicopter, walk across the ramp to the jet, land at Gabreski (FOK), Westhampton Beach — fifteen minutes from Hampton Bays. Door to door, roughly five hours.

Food

A shade house, not a greenhouse

Invert the instinct. In Illinois a greenhouse traps heat. In Guánica it has to shed it. A glass house on that site is an oven.

  • Structure: hot-dip galvanized or concrete frame, 30–50% shade cloth, 50-mesh insect exclusion netting, maximum passive airflow.
  • Hurricane strategy built in: strip the cloth before a storm and the frame rides it out. Sacrificial cladding, permanent bones — how Caribbean growers actually do it.
  • Do not count on evaporative cooling. Guánica is arid in rainfall but coastal; humidity stays high enough that pad-and-fan buys 5–8°F, not the 20 you would get in Arizona.
  • One small sealed, actively cooled room for crops that refuse to cooperate.

Systems: NFT channels for greens and herbs; Dutch buckets on drip for tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, eggplant. EC and pH monitored with automated dosing — the highest-value automation on the property, because a nutrient fault kills a crop in days.

Thrives here: Malabar spinach, callaloo, okra, sweet potato greens, peppers, eggplant, cucumbers, basil, recao. Fights you: lettuce, spinach, brassicas — these live in the cooled room.

Stock a year of nutrient salts. Compact, cheap, and without them the whole system is decorative.

Storage

  • Chest freezers over upright — they hold cold for days without power.
  • Vacuum sealing throughout.
  • A freeze dryer. Vacuum-sealed food lasts one to two years; freeze-dried lasts twenty-five. Large draw, so it is a sun's-out activity — which fits the power profile perfectly.
  • A cold room off the tunnel gallery. Nine hundred feet of buried gallery at stable temperature is already being built; a walk-in down there costs a fraction of one above ground in the tropics. Root-cellar logic with modern refrigeration.
  • A seed bank — sealed, desiccated, refrigerated, open-pollinated varieties only so seed can be saved. The difference between thirty days of autonomy and indefinite.

Cannabis

Home cultivation is a felony, for everyone

Home cultivation is not legal in Puerto Rico, for anyone, including registered patients. Only growers licensed by the Department of Health may cultivate. Legalization bills have been introduced over the years; none had advanced to a vote as of 2026. A personal grow room would be a felony.

What does work: dispensaries. The DOH registry listed about 325 licensed dispensaries as of early 2025, concentrated in San Juan, Ponce and Mayagüez — Ponce is forty minutes away. Patients may purchase a 30-day supply, and Regulation 9038 allows recognition of out-of-state cards. Check current rules on permitted forms; flower has historically been restricted relative to concentrates and vaporization.

A licensed cultivation operation is a real path under Act 42-2017 on eight acres with this security already in place — but it is a regulated commercial enterprise with inspections, tracking and federal banking headaches, not a room off the tunnel.

The conflict that needs a lawyer

Federal law prohibits firearm possession by unlawful users of controlled substances, and ATF treats state-legal medical cannabis patients as unlawful users. Puerto Rico is fully under federal jurisdiction. This compound has an armory. There is active post-Bruen litigation and the ground is genuinely unsettled — but it cannot be resolved in a design document, and the Form 4473 question is not one to be casual about. Get real counsel before holding both a patient card and a firearms licence.

  4. The Zona Marítimo-Terrestre deslinde. MIPR publishes an official reference line along this coast, but a reference line is not a survey. On a parcel whose seaward edge is already conservation-zoned and cut off by a road, this decides how much of Band 0 could ever be used — and nobody can answer it without DRNA. Open — needs DRNA
  5. Elevation profile. USGS 3DEP 1 m, sampled at 2 m stations: the parcel runs +7.2 m to +26.2 m over 240 m, a steady 8% grade. The three-band scheme holds. Closed — USGS 3DEP

  10. Private-property discharge and range operation under Puerto Rico law, plus how insurance changes the moment a non-household member shoots there. Open — needs counsel
  11. The firearms / medical-cannabis federal conflict. Unsettled law, actively litigated, and not resolvable in a design document. Open — needs counsel
